The three clocks that decide when your music stops

Couples usually ask a venue one question: what time does the party finish? The venue gives one number. But there are three separate rules running at the same time, and the earliest one wins.

The noise clock. Catalan noise law defines night as beginning at 23:00. From that hour the permitted level measured at the façade of the nearest dwelling drops steeply. For an isolated, permanently occupied rural dwelling the night limit is 47 dB(A), set out in the annexes to Decret 176/2009. Note where that is measured: not at your dance floor, at your neighbour's window. This is why two venues with identical sound systems can have completely different rules. One has a farmhouse three hundred metres away and the other has a village at the end of the drive.

The outdoor-music clock. This is the one that catches people out. The model municipal ordinance used across Catalonia caps outdoor amplified music at 23:00, and it explicitly covers private installations, so being on private land does not exempt you. It is why so many Catalan venues advertise dinner and the first dance outside, and the party inside. That is not an aesthetic choice.

The licensing clock. Separately, the venue's activity licence sets a closing hour. A licensed banqueting hall in Catalonia typically closes around 02:30, with an extra half hour on Friday and Saturday nights and the eve of public holidays, and a further seasonal extension through the summer that pushes it to roughly 03:30. A mayor can also reduce those hours by up to two hours, though the rule frames this as exceptional and requiring justification, such as documented nuisance or security grounds.

These are the general mechanisms, not a ruling on your venue. Hours are set per venue and per municipality and they change. Always confirm the closing time for your specific date with the venue in writing.

Where these rules come from

So you can check any of this yourself rather than taking our word for it:

  • The night period. Decret 176/2009, which implements Llei 16/2002 on protection against acoustic pollution, sets the assessment periods as day 7h–21h, evening 21h–23h and night 23h–7h.
  • The noise limits. The same decree sets immission limits by zone. For an isolated, permanently occupied rural dwelling, category A3, the night limit is 47 dB(A).
  • Outdoor music. Article 17 of the Generalitat's model municipal noise ordinance sets the operating hours for outdoor amplified music systems, "tant públiques com privades", at 8h–23h on weekdays and 9h–23h at weekends and on holidays. Each municipality adopts its own ordinance, so the one that governs your venue is the local one.
  • Closing times. Ordre INT/358/2011 sets closing for restaurants and salons de banquets at 02:30, with 30 minutes more on Friday and Saturday nights and the eve of holidays, and a further 30 minutes between 1 June and 15 September, at Christmas and Easter, and during local festivals. A mayor may reduce hours by up to two hours in exceptional, justified cases.

These are the general rules. Your venue's actual hours depend on its own licence and its municipality, and they change. Always confirm in writing for your date.

Enclosure predicts your curfew better than anything else

Once you know the three clocks, a pattern falls out that holds across almost every venue we have worked in Catalonia. If the party happens in an enclosed room, you get to somewhere between 03:00 and 03:30. If it happens in the open air, you are realistically looking at midnight to 01:00.

This is why a masia with a restored stone bodega, an old wine cellar or a converted barn is worth more to your night than one with a spectacular terrace and nothing enclosed. The terrace is where you will eat. The cellar is where the wedding actually happens.

There is one honest exception, and it is worth knowing about: a genuinely isolated estate with no dwelling anywhere near it. When there is no neighbour, there is no complaint, and no complaint means no measurement. Those venues are rare and they are the only ones where an outdoor party until late is realistic. You can check this yourself in two minutes on a satellite map before you ever ask the venue.

Who is supplying the sound, and does your DJ know how to work with them

There are three ways sound reaches your dance floor in Catalonia, and they lead to very different conversations.

  • The venue has an installed system or a resident technical company. Common at the larger Catalan estates. It usually means a properly tuned rig and a limiter already set to the licensed level. We come as DJ only and play on their system. This is often the best outcome for you: nothing to hire, nothing to load in, and a technician who already knows the room.
  • You have contracted the technical side separately. Plenty of couples hire an AV or production company for lighting, staging and sound, either directly or through their planner. We work alongside them constantly. They are colleagues, not competition, and a good production company makes a DJ better.
  • Nothing suitable is in the room. Then we supply the sound and lighting ourselves, sized to the space and the guest count rather than to whatever fits in the van.

The reason this matters is that the failure mode is almost never the music. It is finding out at 19:00 that the ceremony speakers do not reach the dance floor, or that the limiter is set for background music, or that nobody was booked to power the terrace. Establishing which of the three applies is a conversation for the planning stage, and we have it before we quote.

Sound limiters, and what actually happens when one trips

Many Catalan venues run a limiter. It sits between the mixing desk and the amplifiers, measures the level in the room, and enforces the licensed maximum. It is sealed and password protected, and in general only a municipal technician can change its setting, which means no amount of charm on the night will move it. Some units log readings every few minutes and retain them for a month. Some cut the mains outright for a fixed period when the level is exceeded, often around twenty seconds. Twenty seconds of silence at 01:30 is a long time.

A limiter is not a problem. It is a constraint, and it rewards a different technique: controlling dynamics, choosing tracks with the energy in the midrange rather than the sub, and getting the perceived loudness from arrangement rather than from level. A DJ who has never worked with one pushes until it trips and then blames the room.

Where in Catalonia we play

Barcelona is the airport and the hotel base. The weddings are mostly somewhere else, within an hour or two of it.

  • Barcelona city. Rooftops, Gothic Quarter palaces, Eixample lofts and hotel terraces. The tightest sound limits anywhere in the region and the shortest outdoor hours, offset by everything being walkable for your guests.
  • The Emporda and the Costa Brava. Restored masias, castles and coastal estates north of Girona. The heart of the international destination-wedding market in Catalonia, and the area with the widest range of curfews.
  • The Penedes. Wine country forty minutes from the city. Cava cellars and working estates, many with the enclosed stone rooms that let a party run late.
  • Sitges and the Garraf. Sea views close to Barcelona, villas and masias in the hills behind the coast. Popular with couples who want the beach without the drive north.
  • Tarragona and the Priorat. Further out, more dramatic, more isolated. Isolation is exactly what buys you a late outdoor party, at the cost of transport and accommodation for everyone.

Frequently asked questions

What time does the music have to stop at a wedding in Catalonia?
There is no single answer, because three separate rules apply at once: the 23:00 legal night period, the outdoor-music cap that is usually also 23:00, and the venue's own licensed closing time of roughly 02:30 to 03:30. The earliest one wins, and a mayor can reduce the licensed hours by up to two hours in exceptional, justified cases. In practice an enclosed room runs to 03:00 and an open-air party stops between midnight and 01:00.

Do we need to hire sound equipment as well as a DJ?
Not always. Some venues have a good installed system or a resident technical company, some couples contract the technical side separately with an AV company, and sometimes there is nothing suitable in the room and we supply it. All three are normal. The point is to know which one applies before the day.

Can we bring our own DJ if the venue has an in-house sound company?
At most Catalan venues, yes. Exclusive AV contracts that block outside performers are rare. What is common is the venue keeping control of the system and the limiter while allowing any DJ or band to play on it. Exclusive catering is far more common than exclusive AV, so the caterer is often the supplier whose approval actually matters.

What is a sound limiter and what happens if it trips?
A sealed device between the desk and the speakers that enforces the licensed level. Only a municipal technician can normally retune it. Some models cut the mains for around twenty seconds when exceeded, which means silence on the dance floor. The job is to programme the night so it never trips.

Do your DJs speak English?
Yes, and we coordinate with your planner, venue and suppliers in English, Spanish or Catalan as needed.

How far in advance should we book?
For peak-season Saturdays from May to September, around twelve months. Off-peak and midweek dates are much more flexible.
