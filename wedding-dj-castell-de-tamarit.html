TAMARIT TARRAGONA

Wedding DJ at
Castell de Tamarit.

A thirteenth-century castle facing the sea gives you almost everything on its own. The one thing it does not give you is a dance floor. It gives you a large open hall, which is a different object, and the floor has to be built inside it. Get that wrong and three hundred people spend the night standing around the edges of a beautiful room.

Tamarit, about 10 minutes from Tarragona, facing the sea in a wooded setting.

A hall is not a dance floor

This is the central problem at castles and it is almost never discussed, because it does not look like a problem in photographs.

A large open-plan room lets guests spread out, and guests will always spread out, because the edges of a room are where the seats, the bar and the conversations are. A dance floor needs the opposite: density. Forty people packed together feels like a party. The same forty distributed across a hall built for three hundred feels like the end of the night, at ten o'clock.

Nothing in a playlist fixes that. What fixes it is the physical arrangement of the room, and it is decided at the planning stage rather than by the DJ at midnight.

  • Give the floor a focal point. The DJ position should be something the room orients towards, not a table pushed against a far wall out of the way.
  • Light the floor and under-light everything else. People move towards light and stay where it is comfortable. If the seating is warmly lit and the dance area is dark, you have told everyone where to sit.
  • Do not let the bar be more attractive than the floor. A bar at the opposite end of a long hall splits the wedding in two, permanently.
  • Put the sound where the dancing should be, rather than spreading it evenly so the whole hall sounds the same. Even coverage is right for a dinner and wrong for a party.

Shrinking the usable space is counterintuitive when you have paid for a castle, and it is the single most effective thing you can do for the night.

Stone, and a very large volume of air

The acoustic problem here is different from a cellar. A vaulted bodega is small, hard and boomy. A castle hall is hard and enormous, which produces a long reverberation tail rather than a build-up of bass: sound keeps arriving after it should have stopped, so speech smears and fast music loses its edges.

The practical consequences are that speeches need more attention than anyone expects, since intelligibility fails long before music does, and that the room rewards tracks with space in them over dense, busy productions that turn to porridge. It also behaves completely differently with three hundred bodies and soft furnishings in it than it does empty at four in the afternoon, which is worth remembering when anyone judges the sound at a quiet soundcheck.

There is a further point that comes with the age of the building. You are working inside a protected structure, so what can be rigged, fixed or hung is limited, and power is what the building has rather than what a modern venue would provide. That is entirely workable and it is a reason to have the technical conversation early rather than to arrive with a plan the walls will not accept.

Where your hours come from

Three Catalan rules run at once and the earliest wins. The legal night begins at 23:00, measured at the façade of the nearest dwelling rather than at your dance floor. Outdoor amplified music is commonly capped at 23:00 by the municipal ordinance, which covers private grounds as well. And the venue's own licence sets a closing time, typically around 02:30 for a licensed banqueting venue with a weekend half hour and a summer extension towards 03:30, which a council may shorten.

What that produces here is the standard and sensible pattern: the gardens by the sea take the ceremony and the aperitif, and the hall takes the party. Plan on that shape. A castle with a genuine enclosed hall is exactly the kind of venue where the rules and the architecture agree with each other.

Licensed hours are set per venue and per municipality and they change from year to year. Confirm the closing time for your own date with the venue in writing.

Bringing your own DJ

The venue presents itself as taking care of the gastronomy, the decoration, the lighting and the music. That is a full-service offer rather than an exclusivity clause, and couples do bring their own DJ, but the two things get confused often enough to be worth separating.

The advice is simple and it is about timing. Raise it when you are booking, not after the contract is signed. Ask for the position in writing. And establish at the same conversation who controls the sound system on the night and whether a limiter is fitted, because that determines whether a visiting DJ arrives with a laptop or with a van.

Venues are almost always relaxed about an external supplier that was agreed at the start and much less relaxed about one that appears as a late change to a signed package. Nothing about that is unreasonable, and it costs you nothing to ask in the right order.

The sea, and the wind that comes with it

An outdoor ceremony on a headland above the water is the reason many couples choose this venue, and it is the most demanding sound situation of the day. There is nothing to reflect sound back towards the guests, so it simply travels away, and the afternoon breeze both carries voices off and pushes a low roar into open microphones.

Turning up does not solve it, because the extra level leaves in the same direction. What solves it is coverage placed close to the guests rather than fired at them from the front, several small sources instead of one loud one, and proper wind protection on every microphone including the celebrant's. If the ceremony is the part that matters most to you, that is where the technical attention belongs.

Getting people home

Tamarit is ten minutes from Tarragona and around an hour and a quarter from Barcelona, with parking on site. For a wedding that runs to the licensed hour, most guests will be on transport, and the same rule applies here as everywhere: book more than one departure. A single coach means everyone leaves at once and turns your last twenty minutes into a queue. Two, thirty to forty minutes apart, lets the night actually finish.

Frequently asked questions

Can we bring our own DJ?
The venue offers music as part of its service, which is not the same as an exclusivity clause. Raise it at booking rather than after signing, get the position in writing, and ask who controls the sound system.

Indoors or outdoors?
Gardens by the sea for the ceremony and aperitif, the hall for the party. That is the Catalan pattern rather than a house preference, because outdoor music is commonly capped at 23:00.

What is difficult about a big hall?
Guests spread out, and a thin dance floor never lifts. Give the floor a focal point, light it and under-light the rest, keep the bar close, and aim the sound at the floor rather than the room.

How do guests get there?
Ten minutes from Tarragona, around an hour and a quarter from Barcelona, Reus airport closer, parking on site. Book two departures rather than one.
