There is one fact about getting married here that almost nobody tells international couples, and it changes the shape of the entire day: the legal night starts at 22:00 in the Comunitat Valenciana, a full hour earlier than in Catalonia. If you have been comparing a Valencia venue with a Barcelona one, you have not been comparing like with like. Here is what that actually means for your party.

The hour that changes everything

Noise law in Spain is regional, not national. Each autonomous community sets its own definition of the night period, and the Comunitat Valenciana puts it at 22:00. Catalonia puts it at 23:00. That is not a technicality. It is the hour at which the permitted noise level measured at the nearest home drops sharply, and everything about an outdoor evening has to be built around it.

Note where the measurement happens. Not on your dance floor, and not at the venue boundary: at the façade of the nearest dwelling. Which is why the real question about any Valencian venue is not what sound system it has. It is how far away the closest house is, and whether anyone lives in it.

The practical consequence is that a Valencian wedding day is compressed at the front. Ceremony, aperitif and dinner outside works beautifully here, and the region is close to unbeatable for it. But if the plan is that everyone stays on the terrace until three in the morning, the plan needs revisiting early, while you can still choose a venue that supports it.

Enclosure predicts your curfew

Across this region the pattern is unusually consistent. An enclosed party room gets you to 03:00 or later. An open-air party ends between midnight and 01:00. We have not yet found a venue in the area that breaks it, and a good number publish exactly these two numbers side by side on their own websites: music outside until 22:00, party inside until 03:00.

Separately from the noise rules, the venue's activity licence sets a closing hour. A licensed banqueting venue in the Comunitat Valenciana can generally run to around 03:30, and local councils have the power to shorten that. So when a venue quotes you a finishing time, it is quoting the earliest of several rules, and that number is specific to that venue in that municipality.

These are the general mechanisms, not a ruling on your venue. Closing hours are set per venue and per municipality and they change from year to year. Confirm the hours for your specific date with the venue, in writing.

Limiters on the Costa Blanca

Sound limiters are common here and some towns enforce them hard. The unit sits between the mixing desk and the amplifiers, measures the level and enforces the licensed maximum. It is sealed and password protected, so nobody at the wedding can change it. Some models do not simply reduce the volume when exceeded, they cut the mains for a fixed period, often around twenty seconds. Some installations log readings continuously and report to the council in real time.

None of this is a reason for a worse party. It is a reason to hire someone who has worked inside it. Getting a room to feel loud under a limiter is a technique: control the dynamics, pick tracks whose energy lives in the midrange rather than the sub, and build the lift from arrangement rather than from level. A DJ meeting a limiter for the first time pushes until it trips, and then the room goes silent at the worst possible moment.

Three kinds of room, three different jobs

The Comunitat Valenciana has more purpose-built wedding venues than almost anywhere in Spain. Whole estates exist to do nothing but weddings, forty weekends a year. That shapes the technical side in a way it does not in Catalonia, where a lot of venues are old farmhouses adapted afterwards.

  • The purpose-built salón. Designed as a banqueting hall, with an installed system, a limiter already set to the licensed level and often a resident technician who knows the room better than any visiting supplier will. Here we come as DJ only and play on their rig, which is usually the best result for everyone.
  • The marquee. Extremely common on Valencian fincas and its own particular problem. Fabric absorbs almost nothing at low frequencies and reflects at high ones, so a marquee is simultaneously boomy and harsh, and its walls stop essentially no sound from reaching the neighbours. A marquee is not an indoor room in either the acoustic or the legal sense, whatever it feels like from inside.
  • The open terrace or garden. Beautiful, and the format the 22:00 rule was written for. This needs coverage spread across the space rather than volume from one end, because there are no walls to help you and every decibel you add goes straight over the wall to the nearest house.

Whichever it is, somebody has to supply the sound: the venue, an AV company you have contracted separately, or us. All three happen constantly here and none is a problem. The problem is when nobody establishes which, and the answer turns out to be nobody.

Outdoor ceremonies need singling out, because this region has more of them than anywhere else we work and they are the most under-planned element of a Costa Blanca wedding. Open sky, no walls and an afternoon sea breeze mean a ceremony needs its own coverage and its own microphones, not a speaker carried over from the dinner setup twenty minutes beforehand.

We build the timeline backwards from 22:00

That is the practical difference in how we plan a wedding here. In Catalonia you can design the evening forwards and the outdoor cut-off arrives late enough to feel like a natural transition. In this region 22:00 lands in the middle of dinner, so the day has to be laid out in reverse from it: when the ceremony starts, how long the aperitif runs, when the first course is served and when the party gets its move indoors.

Get that right and nobody at the wedding ever notices there was a rule. Get it wrong and the music stops at the exact moment the evening was starting to work, everyone stands around, and the party never fully recovers. It is the most common failure at Valencian weddings and it is entirely a planning problem, not a musical one.

Frequently asked questions

What time does the music have to stop at a wedding in Valencia or on the Costa Blanca?
Two clocks run at once: the legal night period begins at 22:00, and the venue's licence typically allows opening until around 03:30, which a council can shorten. In practice an enclosed room runs to 03:00 or later and an open-air party ends between midnight and 01:00.

Why is the curfew earlier here than in Barcelona?
Noise law is regional. The Comunitat Valenciana starts the legal night at 22:00, Catalonia at 23:00. That hour changes how the evening is programmed, so a Valencia venue and a Barcelona venue are not directly comparable.

Can we have the party outside?
Dinner, ceremony and the early evening outside is one of the great pleasures of this region. A full outdoor party until late depends almost entirely on how far the nearest home is. Isolated estates are the exception rather than the rule, and it is worth checking on a satellite map early.

Do Valencian venues use sound limiters?
Many do. They are sealed and password protected, some cut the mains for a fixed period when exceeded, and some report to the council continuously. The job is to programme the night so the limiter never trips.

Do we need to hire sound equipment as well as a DJ?
Not always. Some venues have their own system or technical company, some couples contract AV separately, and sometimes we supply everything. All three are normal here.

Do your DJs speak English?
Yes, and we coordinate with your planner, venue and suppliers in English or Spanish.
