Wedding DJ at Mas Torroella

A 14th-century manor on a private estate in the Emporda, and one of a tiny handful of Costa Brava venues where the clock is not the thing that ends your wedding. Here is what that actually buys you, and what to do with it.

Why "no curfew" is such an unusual line

Almost every wedding venue in Catalonia has an end time, and most of them do not publish it. On the Costa Brava specifically, the pattern is remarkably consistent: outdoor amplified music stops around 22:00 or 23:00, the party moves indoors, and the venue's licence closes the night somewhere between 02:30 and 03:30.

That pattern exists for a reason. Catalan municipalities set a hard stop on outdoor amplified music, and the limits that apply after that are measured not at the venue but at the nearest neighbouring dwelling. In rural Catalonia the night-time limit at an isolated house can be as low as 47 dB, which is roughly conversation at a few metres. It is the neighbour, not the clock, that usually decides how loud a masia wedding can be.

Which explains why a venue like this one is different. Mas Torroella is a private estate taken on exclusive use, and it does not publish its address. No shared road, no adjacent village, no neighbour standing in a garden at midnight with a phone. Independent venue directories list it with no music curfew, and in our own experience playing there we ran into no sound restriction at all.

Licensed hours can be varied by the town hall and do change. Whatever any guide tells you, including this one, get the position for your own date from the estate in writing before you plan around it.

What to actually do with a night that does not have an end

Here is the counterintuitive part, and it is the reason this page exists rather than just saying "no curfew, great".

A wedding with no end time is harder to programme well than one that finishes at three. When there is a hard stop, the shape is obvious: you build toward it and you finish on the biggest song you have. Remove the stop and most weddings sag. The floor thins out around two, people drift, and what should have been the best two hours of the night becomes a long tail of twenty people and a playlist.

What works instead is treating it as two parties rather than one long one.

  • Build a real peak and land it. Somewhere around 01:30 the wedding should hit its highest point and everybody should feel it. Do not save energy for later on the theory that there is time.
  • Then change the room. Different tempo, different lighting, ideally a visible reset. The people who stay have chosen to stay, and they want something other than more of the same.
  • Let the second half be looser. This is where the requests, the deep cuts and the things that would never work at midnight actually land.
  • Have an ending in mind anyway. An open-ended night still needs a last song. Deciding it at 4am rarely produces a good one.

None of that costs anything. It just has to be decided in advance rather than improvised at two in the morning.

The multi-day factor

Mas Torroella is taken on exclusive use with a multi-night minimum, which in practice makes this a wedding weekend rather than a wedding day. Around 28 to 30 people sleep on the estate, with the rest of the guest list staying nearby.

That changes the music brief in a way couples often only realise late. You are not booking one evening, you are potentially covering a welcome dinner, a pool or garden session the following day, and the wedding itself, each of which wants something completely different. A daytime set by the pool and a 2am set in an enclosed hall have almost nothing in common beyond the person playing them.

It is worth deciding early which of those moments you actually want covered. Some couples want all of them handled. Others want the wedding done properly and are happy with a speaker and a playlist for the rest. Both are reasonable, but they are very different bookings.

Open vendor, and why that is rarer than it sounds

Most premium Catalan venues lock catering. A significant number also route AV through a single in-house or preferred supplier. Mas Torroella does neither: no exclusive caterer, no imposed sound company, a recommended list rather than a mandate.

That freedom is genuinely valuable, but it comes with one obligation. When nobody is appointed to own the technical side, somebody still has to. On an isolated estate that means confirming, well before the week of the wedding, where the power is coming from and whether it is adequate, who is building the PA and lighting in the events hall, what the load-in route and vehicle access actually are, and who is on site during setup to make decisions.

None of it is complicated. It just has nobody automatically assigned to it, which is exactly how it ends up unresolved.
