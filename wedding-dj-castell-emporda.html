Ten o'clock is not a suggestion

The single most important thing to understand about a wedding here is that outdoor music stops at 22:00. Not fades, not turns down. Stops.

This is not the hotel being difficult. Catalan municipalities cap outdoor amplified music, typically at 22:00 or 23:00 depending on the town, and the rule explicitly covers private installations, so a venue's own terrace speakers are caught by it. The venue holds the licence and the venue pays the fine, which is why these limits tend to be enforced more strictly than the law technically requires.

What that means in practice is that everything happening outdoors, the ceremony, the drinks, the dinner, the speeches, the first dance if you want it under the sky, has to be finished and wrapped by ten. Every couple who has a difficult evening here has one for the same reason: the timeline slipped by forty minutes over the afternoon and nobody moved the ten o'clock deadline, so the transition happened in a rush with half the guests still at the table.

Build the running order backwards from 22:00, not forwards from the ceremony. Then take fifteen minutes out as slack. Spanish weddings run late by default and this is the one deadline that will not move for you.

The cave changes what the second half can be

Then the good part. La Cova del Drac is not a themed basement, it is an actual cave underneath a fourteenth-century castle, used as a lounge bar and dance floor.

Acoustically it is the most contained party space we have worked in on this coast. Stone and earth on every side means essentially nothing escapes, which is precisely why the party can continue there after the terrace has gone silent. It is the clearest example anywhere on the Costa Brava of the rule that governs this whole region: outdoor is limited by the municipality, indoor is limited only by the venue's licence.

It also means the second half of the wedding is a genuinely different event from the first, and that should be deliberate rather than accidental. A cave is intimate, warm, loud and close. It does not want the same set as an open terrace at sunset, and it rewards a real gear change at the door rather than a continuation.

Two practical notes from having worked the room. Stone reflects everything, so the room is far less forgiving of excess volume than an open terrace, and pushing it makes the mix worse rather than bigger. And the walk down is a natural reset, so it is worth using: a deliberate first track for the cave lands much harder than simply carrying on.

The limiter, and what it actually does

There is a sound limitation in place here, which is standard for an enclosed party space in Catalonia and worth understanding rather than fearing.

A Catalan sound limiter is a sealed device wired between the mains and the playback rig. It is programmed by an authorised technician and physically sealed or password-locked, so neither the venue nor the DJ can legally adjust it. It logs levels continuously, it can hold different thresholds for different times of night, and if it is disconnected from the mains or from its microphone the music stops. Some configurations also detect additional sound sources running in parallel, so bringing a spare speaker is not a workaround.

If the threshold is exceeded, the typical behaviour is that power to the playback system is cut for a period, commonly around twenty seconds. Twenty seconds of silence at midnight empties a dance floor, and it takes considerably longer than twenty seconds to get it back.

The answer is not to fight it. It is to know the number in advance and mix for it. A set built for a fixed ceiling sounds better than one that keeps hitting the ceiling and dropping out, and no guest has ever noticed a well-managed limiter. They notice the silences.

Who supplies the sound, and the condition to check

AV at the venue is handled by a sound company. That is not a problem for your choice of DJ or band, and in many ways it is an advantage: the rig is already specified for the space and somebody who knows the cave is building it.

What does need checking is the wider supplier policy. External catering is not permitted, and external suppliers are generally accepted conditional on booking the hotel's catering and rooms. That is a normal arrangement for a castle hotel with 57 rooms, but it is a condition rather than an open door, and it is much better understood before you sign anything than after.

Two questions to put to the hotel in writing, early:

  • What is the indoor end time for our exact date, and does it change on a Saturday or in high season.
  • What is the limiter set to, and who do we speak to about the rig in the cave. Then put that person in touch with whoever is playing.

One more thing that is not about sound at all: the castle sits on a hill, so guest shuttles are worth arranging, and that includes the end of the night. A cave party that runs late is only a good idea if there is a way home from it.
